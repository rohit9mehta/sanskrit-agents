## Justifications

**J1. Anejat means not merely ‘not moving’ but remaining always of one unaltered form, without departure from its own state.**
- chosen: unmoving and intrinsically unchanging (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [1, 2]: `anejat na ejat / ejṛ kampane, kampanaṃ calanaṃ svāvasthāpracyutistadvarjitaṃ sarvadaikarūpamityarthaḥ /` — ‘Anejat’ means ‘not moving.’ Ej is used of trembling or motion, namely departure from one's own state; it is devoid of that and always of one form.

**J2. Ekam denotes the one Self-principle in all beings, while manasaḥ is an ablative of comparison with javīyaḥ.**
- chosen: the one Self, swifter than the mind (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [3]: `taccaikaṃ sarvabhūteṣu manasaḥ saṅkalpādilakṣaṇād javīyo javavattaram /` — That is one in all beings and is swifter—possessed of greater speed—than the mind characterized by volition and the like.

**J3. The apparent contradiction between being unmoving and swifter than mind is resolved by distinguishing the Self without limiting adjuncts from its appearance in association with the mind as an adjunct.**
- chosen: intrinsic changelessness and adjunct-conditioned apparent precedence (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [4, 5]: `kathaṃ viruddhamucyate dhruvaṃ niścalamidaṃ manaso javīya iti ca / naiṣa doṣaḥ /` — How can the contradictory claims be made that it is fixed and motionless and that it is swifter than the mind? This is no fault.
- isup [6]: `nirupādhyupādhimattvenopapatteḥ tatra nirupādhikena svena rūpeṇocyate anejadekamiti manaso 'ntaḥkaraṇasya saṅkalpavikalpalakṣaṇasyopādheranuvarttanād iha dehasthasya manaso brahmalokādidūragamanaṃ saṅkalpena kṣaṇamātrādbhavatītyato manaso javiṣṭhatvaṃ loke prasiddham /` — The statements are coherent through the distinction between being without and with an adjunct. ‘Unmoving, one’ describes its own unconditioned nature; the mind, the internal organ characterized by determination and doubt, is its limiting adjunct and is conventionally regarded as exceedingly swift.
- isup [7]: `tasmin manasi brahmalokādīn drutaṃ gacchati sati prathamaṃ prāpta ivātmacaitanyāvabhāso gṛhyate 'to manaso javīya ityāha /` — When the mind travels swiftly to Brahmā's world and elsewhere, the manifestation of the Self's consciousness is apprehended as though it had arrived first; hence it is called swifter than mind.

**J4. Devāḥ denotes the senses, not independent mythological gods; enat is their accusative object, the Self-principle that they failed to reach.**
- chosen: the luminous senses could not reach or objectify the Self (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [8]: `nainaddevā dyotanāddevāścakṣurādīnīndriyāṇyetatprakṛtamātmatattvaṃ nāpnuvanna prāptavantaḥ /` — The ‘deities,’ so called from their illumination, are the senses beginning with sight; they did not attain this Self-principle under discussion.
- isup [9]: `tebhyo mano javīyaḥ manovyāpāravyavahitatvād ābhāsamātramapi ātmano naiva devānāṃ viṣayībhavati /` — Mind is swifter than they; because the senses operate only through the mind, not even a mere appearance of the Self becomes their object.

**J5. Pūrvam is adverbial, and arṣat is construed as a finite Vedic augmentless past form (injunctive): the Self had already ‘gone’ before even the swift mind, figuratively because it is all-pervading.**
- chosen: finite augmentless ‘it had already gone before,’ explained through omnipresence (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [10]: `yasmājjavanānmanaso 'pi pūrvamarṣat pūrvameva gataṃ vyomavadvyāpitvāt` — For it went before even the swift mind—it had already gone, because it is all-pervading like space.

**J6. Tat is the nominative subject of atyeti; dhāvataḥ anyān is its accusative object. The ‘other runners’ are mind, speech, senses, and similar non-Self factors.**
- chosen: tat as subject and dhāvataḥ anyān as accusative plural object (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [11]: `taddhāvato drutaṃ gacchato 'nyānātmavilakṣaṇānmanovāgindriyaprabhṛtīnatyeti atītya gacchati iva /` — It passes beyond, as though going beyond, the other rapidly moving things distinct from the Self—mind, speech, senses, and so forth.

**J7. Atyeti does not imply real alteration or locomotion by the Self. Tiṣṭhat supplies the intended ‘as though’: it seems to outstrip the runners while itself remaining changeless.**
- chosen: the Self remains intrinsically changeless but appears to pass beyond the runners (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [11]: `atyeti atītya gacchati iva /` — It is as though it goes after passing beyond them.
- isup [12]: `ivārthaṃ svayameva darśayati tiṣṭhaditi svayamavikriyameva sadityarthaḥ /` — The word tiṣṭhat itself indicates the sense ‘as though’: it itself remains entirely changeless.

**J8. Tasmin locates all activity in the Self-principle as its conscious, universal substrate; it does not make the Self an agent undergoing those activities.**
- chosen: in that Self as the abiding substrate (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [13]: `tasminnātmatattve sati nityacaitanyasvabhāve` — When that Self-principle, whose nature is eternal consciousness, exists.
- isup [17]: `sarvā hi kāryakaraṇādivikriyā nityacaitanyātmasvarūpe sarvāspadabhūte satyeva bhavantītyarthaḥ` — All transformations of effects, instruments, and the like occur only while the eternally conscious Self, the universal substrate, exists.

**J9. Mātariśvan is identified here as Vāyu, the Wind—the vital and cosmic power sustaining embodied beings and their instruments.**
- chosen: Vāyu as the life-sustaining cosmic activity-principle (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [13]: `mātariśvā vāyuḥ sarvaprāṇabhṛt kriyātmako` — Mātariśvan is Vāyu, the Wind, sustaining all living beings and having the nature of activity.

**J10. Apaḥ is translated as ‘activities,’ not ‘waters’: it includes the movements of living beings and the operations of fire, sun, rain, and other natural powers.**
- chosen: activities and operations (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [13]: `apaḥ karmāṇi prāṇināṃ ceṣṭālakṣaṇāni, agnyādityaparjanyādīnāṃ jvalanadahanaprakāśābhivarṣaṇādilakṣaṇāni` — Apaḥ means activities: the movements of living beings and such functions of fire, sun, rain, and the like as burning, consuming, illuminating, and raining.

**J11. Dadhāti is rendered by Śaṅkara's primary gloss vibhajati, ‘apportions’ or ‘distributes.’ He records ‘supports’ as an alternative, but that is not the primary translation adopted here.**
- chosen: apportions or distributes (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [13]: `dadhāti vibhajati ityarthaḥ /` — Dadhāti means ‘distributes.’
- isup [14, 15, 16]: `dhārayatīti vā"bhīṣāsmādvātaḥ pavate" (tai. u. 2 / 8 / 1) ityādi śrutibhyaḥ /` — Alternatively, it means ‘supports,’ as indicated by scriptural passages such as ‘From fear of him the wind blows.’ The quotation numbering and spacing reflect the unproofread source.

## Translation

> Unmoving, the one Self (ātman) is swifter than the mind;
the sense-deities (devāḥ) did not reach it—it had gone before.
Though standing still, it seems to outstrip the others as they run;
in it Mātariśvan (the Wind) apportions activities (apaḥ).

## Analyzer disagreements

- arṣat: the analyzer labels it a neuter accusative singular present participle. It is instead analyzed as a finite Vedic augmentless imperfect (injunctive), third-person singular Kartari; Śaṅkara paraphrases pūrvam arṣat with pūrvam eva gatam (line 10). The lakāra is left null because the schema lacks an Injunctive value and ordinary Lan derives the augmented ārṣat, not transmitted arṣat.
- tat: the analyzer selects neuter accusative singular. It is nominative singular, the subject of atyeti; the nominative and accusative forms are syncretic, but the syntax and commentary at line 11 require the nominative construal.
- tiṣṭhat: the analyzer selects neuter accusative singular. It is nominative singular agreeing with subject tat; Śaṅkara explains that it indicates the Self itself remains changeless (line 12).

## One-shot delta

- A commentary-blind translation would likely take devāḥ as mythological gods, whereas Śaṅkara explicitly identifies them as the senses beginning with sight.
- It could translate apaḥ as ‘waters’; Śaṅkara instead defines it as the activities of living beings and the operations of natural powers.
- It could attribute literal rapid motion to the Self. Śaṅkara distinguishes the changeless, unconditioned Self from its apparent precedence through the moving mind and reads an implicit ‘as though’ with atyeti.
- It might identify Mātariśvan with Agni or merely with atmospheric air, whereas the commentary identifies him as Vāyu, the life-sustaining cosmic activity-principle.
- It could take arṣat as a participle; Śaṅkara's pūrvam eva gatam supports a finite augmentless past construal, with the apparent prior ‘going’ explained by omnipresence.
