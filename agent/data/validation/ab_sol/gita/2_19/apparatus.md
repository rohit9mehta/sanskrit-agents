## Justifications

**J1. The pronouns enam and ayam denote the presently discussed Self (ātman), described as the embodied one (dehin), not the physical body itself.**
- chosen: the embodied Self (ātman) (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [1]: `ya enaṃ prakṛtaṃ dehinaṃ` — ‘Whoever [knows] this presently discussed embodied one.’
- gita [2]: `avivekena ātmānam` — ‘The Self, through lack of discrimination.’

**J2. Hantāram and hatam are predicative accusatives qualifying enam: the first presents the Self as agent of killing, while the second presents it as the patient who has been slain.**
- chosen: predicative accusatives: ‘as the slayer’ and ‘as slain’ (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [1]: `hantāraṃ hanana-kriyāyāḥ kartāraṃ ya ca enam anyo manyate hataṃ deha-hananena hato 'ham iti hanana-kriyāyāḥ karma-bhūtam` — ‘Hantāram: the agent of the act of killing; and another regards this as slain—“I have been slain through the killing of the body”—as having become the patient of the act of killing.’

**J3. Ubhau tau gathers the subjects of both relative clauses into one dual subject, and na vijānītaḥ denies them genuine understanding of the Self rather than all cognition whatsoever.**
- chosen: both persons fail to understand the Self’s true nature (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [2]: `tau ubhau na vijānītaḥ na jñātavantau avivekena ātmānam` — ‘Those two, both, do not understand—have not known—the Self, because of non-discrimination.’
- gita [3]: `tau ātma-svarūpānabhijñau ity arthaḥ` — ‘The meaning is that those two are unacquainted with the Self’s true nature.’

**J4. Hanti is active and denies the Self the status of agent; hanyate is passive and denies it the status of patient. Both denials are grounded in the Self’s immutability.**
- chosen: ‘neither slays nor is slain,’ denying agency and patienthood (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [4]: `hanti na hanana-kriyāyāḥ kartā bhavati, na ca hanyate na ca karma bhavatīty arthaḥ, avikriyatvāt` — 
- gita [4]: `hanti na hanana-kriyāyāḥ kartā bhavati, na ca hanyate na ca karma bhavatīty arthaḥ, avikriyatvāt` — ‘[It] slays not: it does not become the agent of the act of killing; nor is it slain, and so does not become the patient—the meaning being that it is immutable.’

**J5. The two mistaken judgments are first-person identifications—‘I am the slayer’ and ‘I have been slain’—arising when bodily killing is superimposed upon the Self.**
- chosen: first-person misidentification caused by failure to discriminate body and Self (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [3]: `hantā ahaṃ, hato 'smy aham iti deha-hananena ātmānam ahaṃ pratyaya-viṣayaṃ yau vijānītaḥ` — ‘The two who cognize the Self, the object of the “I”-notion, as “I am the slayer” and “I have been slain,” on account of the killing of the body.’

## Translation

> Whoever knows this Self (ātman) as the slayer, and whoever regards it as slain—
both of them fail to understand: this Self neither slays nor is slain.

## One-shot delta

- A commentary-blind translation might take enam and ayam as referring to the physical body; Śaṅkara identifies their referent as the Self and treats bodily killing as the source of the error.
- It might read the first two clauses as valid descriptions, whereas the commentary says that both thinkers lack discrimination and do not know the Self’s true nature.
- It might render hanyate merely as ‘does not perish’ or as a middle form; the commentary specifically construes it passively and denies that the Self is the patient of killing.
- It might miss the first-person force of the error: Śaṅkara expands the judgments as ‘I am the slayer’ and ‘I have been slain.’
