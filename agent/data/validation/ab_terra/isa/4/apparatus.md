## Justifications

**J1. anejat means “unmoving,” namely free from all movement and from departure from its own condition; it is not a verbal assertion that the Self does not merely ‘vibrate.’**
- chosen: “Unmoving.” (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [1, 2]: `anejat na ejat / ejṛ kampane, kampanaṃ calanaṃ svāvasthāpracyutistadvarjitaṃ sarvadaikarūpamityarthaḥ /` — ‘Anejat’ means ‘not moving.’ The root ej denotes trembling; trembling is motion and departure from one’s own state. The meaning is that which is devoid of this and always of one uniform nature.

**J2. ekaṃ is the one Self present in all beings, and javīyaḥ (“swifter than mind”) does not attribute physical locomotion to it. The comparison is explained through the mind’s rapid imaginative travel and the Self-consciousness that is apprehended as already present there.**
- chosen: “One, swifter than the mind,” with the swiftness understood as apparent priority rather than literal motion. (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [3, 6, 7]: `taccaikaṃ sarvabhūteṣu manasaḥ saṅkalpādilakṣaṇād javīyo javavattaram /` — And it is one in all beings, swifter—more rapid—than the mind, characterized by volition and the like.
- isup [6]: `nirupādhyupādhimattvenopapatteḥ tatra nirupādhikena svena rūpeṇocyate anejadekamiti` — Because the two descriptions are compatible when viewed as free from limiting adjuncts and as possessing them, ‘unmoving, one’ is said of it in its own nature free from adjuncts.
- isup [7]: `tasmin manasi brahmalokādīn drutaṃ gacchati sati prathamaṃ prāpta ivātmacaitanyāvabhāso gṛhyate 'to manaso javīya ityāha /` — When the mind swiftly goes to Brahmaloka and the like, the manifestation of the Self’s consciousness is apprehended as though it had arrived first; therefore the text says that it is swifter than the mind.

**J3. devāḥ denotes the illuminating sense-organs, not gods; they cannot make the Self their object. pūrvam arṣat says that the Self has already ‘gone before’ only in the sense that it is all-pervasive.**
- chosen: “The senses did not overtake It, for It had gone before.” (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [8, 9]: `nainaddevā dyotanāddevāścakṣurādīnīndriyāṇyetatprakṛtamātmatattvaṃ nāpnuvanna prāptavantaḥ / tebhyo mano javīyaḥ manovyāpāravyavahitatvād ābhāsamātramapi ātmano naiva devānāṃ viṣayībhavati /` — ‘The devas,’ so called from illuminating, are the sense-organs beginning with the eye; they did not attain this Self-principle under discussion. The mind is swifter than they, yet, because it is mediated by mental activity, even a mere manifestation of the Self does not become an object for the senses.
- isup [10]: `yasmājjavanānmanaso 'pi pūrvamarṣat pūrvameva gataṃ vyomavadvyāpitvāt sarvavyāpi tadātmatattva` — Because it had gone before even the swiftly moving mind—had indeed gone previously—owing to its sky-like pervasiveness, that Self-principle is all-pervasive.

**J4. The ‘others’ that the Self outstrips are entities distinct from it—mind, speech, senses, and so on. atyeti is explicitly only an apparent passing-beyond: while all these run, the Self itself remains changeless, as tiṣṭhat states.**
- chosen: “Though It stands still, It outstrips the other runners.” (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [11, 12]: `taddhāvato drutaṃ gacchato 'nyānātmavilakṣaṇānmanovāgindriyaprabhṛtīnatyeti atītya gacchati iva / ivārthaṃ svayameva darśayati tiṣṭhaditi svayamavikriyameva sadityarthaḥ /` — That outstrips the others—distinct from the Self, beginning with mind, speech, and the senses—which go very swiftly; it goes beyond them, as it were. The word ‘as it were’ is itself indicated by tiṣṭhat: it means that it remains itself wholly changeless.

**J5. Mātariśvan is Wind, and apaḥ means actions or functions, not waters. dadhāti is therefore “apportions/distributes” those activities within the Self, their changeless support.**
- chosen: “In It Mātariśvan, the Wind, apportions actions.” (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [13]: `tasminnātmatattve sati nityacaitanyasvabhāve mātariśvā vāyuḥ sarvaprāṇabhṛt kriyātmako` — When that Self-principle, whose nature is eternal consciousness, is present, Mātariśvan is Wind, bearing all living beings and of the nature of activity.
- isup [13]: `apaḥ karmāṇi prāṇināṃ ceṣṭālakṣaṇāni, agnyādityaparjanyādīnāṃ jvalanadahanaprakāśābhivarṣaṇādilakṣaṇāni dadhāti vibhajati ityarthaḥ /` — Apaḥ are actions: the activities of living beings, and the burning, consuming, shining, raining, and similar functions of fire, sun, rain-cloud, and so on. ‘Dadhāti’ means ‘distributes.’
- isup [17]: `sarvā hi kāryakaraṇādivikriyā nityacaitanyātmasvarūpe sarvāspadabhūte satyeva bhavantītyarthaḥ` — For all modifications of effects, instruments, and the like occur only when the eternal-consciousness nature of the Self, the ground of all, is present.

## Translation

> Unmoving, one, swifter than the mind: the senses did not overtake It, for It had gone before. Though It stands still, It outstrips the other runners; in It Mātariśvan—the Wind—apportions actions.

## Analyzer disagreements

- The analyzer separates anejat into an and ejat. I analyze an- as the privative prefix in the nañ-tatpuruṣa anejat, following the explicit gloss “anejat na ejat” (lines 1–2), rather than as an independent verbal item.
- The analyzer’s local tag for arṣat is nonfinite/nominal in form. arṣat is instead a finite Vedic augmentless imperfect (injunctive), third-person singular active, from ṛṣ; it is not a luṅ/aorist form.
- The analyzer labels apaḥ as feminine plural, which would yield ‘waters.’ I override this: it is neuter accusative plural from Vedic ap ‘work,’ because Śaṅkara explicitly says “apaḥ karmāṇi” (line 13).

## One-shot delta

- A commentary-blind translation may take devāḥ as gods rather than the eye and other sense-organs, and so miss the epistemological claim that the Self is not an object of sensory cognition.
- It may render apaḥ as “waters”; Śaṅkara expressly defines it as karmāṇi, the activities of beings and cosmic agencies.
- It may construe the verse as attributing actual racing and overtaking to Brahman. Śaṅkara makes both the prior ‘going’ and the ‘outstripping’ figurative, while tiṣṭhat asserts intrinsic changelessness.
