## Justifications

**J1. anejat is not a separate finite clause ‘it does not move’ but a neuter adjectival negative formation meaning ‘unmoving,’ and ekaṃ marks the same one ātman present in all beings.**
- chosen: Read anejat as ‘unmoving,’ the privative of ejat ‘moving/shaking,’ and ekaṃ as ‘one’ with reference to the single ātmatattva in all beings. (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [1, 2]: `anejat na ejat / ejṛ kampane, kampanaṃ calanaṃ svāvasthāpracyutistadvarjitaṃ sarvadaikarūpamityarthaḥ /` — ‘anejat’ means ‘not ejat’; √ej is shaking, and shaking is motion, a lapse from one’s own state; the meaning is that it is devoid of that and always of one form.
- isup [3]: `taccaikaṃ sarvabhūteṣu` — And that is one in all beings.

**J2. The apparent contradiction between ‘unmoving’ and ‘swifter than mind’ is resolved by Śaṅkara through the distinction between the unconditioned Self and its appearance with limiting adjuncts.**
- chosen: Translate both predicates, but understand the speed-predicate as due to mental upādhi and apparent manifestation, not real change in the Self. (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [4, 5, 6]: `kathaṃ viruddhamucyate dhruvaṃ niścalamidaṃ manaso javīya iti ca / naiṣa doṣaḥ / nirupādhyupādhimattvenopapatteḥ` — Objection: how can the fixed and motionless be called swifter than mind? This is no fault, because it is intelligible as unconditioned and as possessed of limiting adjuncts.
- isup [6, 7]: `manaso 'ntaḥkaraṇasya saṅkalpavikalpalakṣaṇasyopādheranuvarttanād ... tasmin manasi brahmalokādīn drutaṃ gacchati sati prathamaṃ prāpta ivātmacaitanyāvabhāso gṛhyate 'to manaso javīya ityāha /` — Because it follows the upādhi, the internal organ called mind, characterized by resolve and doubt; when that mind goes swiftly even to Brahmaloka and the like, the manifestation of Self-consciousness is grasped as though it had arrived first—therefore the text says ‘swifter than mind.’

**J3. manasaḥ is an ablative of comparison with javīyaḥ, and ‘mind’ is specifically the saṅkalpa/vikalpa-type internal organ.**
- chosen: Read ‘manaso javīyaḥ’ as ‘swifter than the mind,’ where mind is the internal organ of ideation. (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [3]: `manasaḥ saṅkalpādilakṣaṇād javīyo javavattaram` — ‘Swifter than the mind’—than mind characterized by resolve and the like; more possessed of speed.
- isup [6]: `manaso 'ntaḥkaraṇasya saṅkalpavikalpalakṣaṇasya` — The mind is the internal organ characterized by resolve and doubt.

**J4. devāḥ here are not translated simply as mythic gods; Śaṅkara identifies them as the luminous sense-faculties, eye and the rest, which cannot attain the Self.**
- chosen: Translate as ‘deva-s, the shining senses,’ and take enat as the already introduced Self-principle which they do not reach. (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [8, 9]: `nainaddevā dyotanāddevāścakṣurādīnīndriyāṇyetatprakṛtamātmatattvaṃ nāpnuvanna prāptavantaḥ / tebhyo mano javīyaḥ manovyāpāravyavahitatvād ābhāsamātramapi ātmano naiva devānāṃ viṣayībhavati /` — The deva-s are so called from shining: the sense-organs beginning with the eye. They did not attain this Self-principle under discussion. Since mind is swifter than they, and since the operation of mind intervenes, not even a mere appearance of the Self becomes an object for the deva-s.

**J5. pūrvam arṣat is a participial predication of the Self: ‘already gone before,’ grounded in the Self’s all-pervasiveness, not a finite Laṅ form.**
- chosen: Read arṣat morphologically as a neuter nominative singular participial form from √ṛṣ; in context, with Śaṅkara’s gloss, translate the predication as ‘already gone before.’ (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [10]: `yasmājjavanānmanaso 'pi pūrvamarṣat pūrvameva gataṃ vyomavadvyāpitvāt sarvavyāpi tadātmatattva` — Because, even before the swift mind, it was ‘arṣat’—already gone before—since, like space, it is all-pervasive; that Self-principle is all-pervasive.

**J6. dhāvataḥ anyān is accusative plural: the ‘other’ runners are mind, speech, senses, etc.; atyeti is qualified by Śaṅkara’s iva because the Self itself remains stationary.**
- chosen: Read ‘tat dhāvataḥ anyān atyeti tiṣṭhat’ as: ‘That, while standing, as it were outstrips the other runners.’ (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [11, 12]: `taddhāvato drutaṃ gacchato 'nyānātmavilakṣaṇānmanovāgindriyaprabhṛtīnatyeti atītya gacchati iva / ivārthaṃ svayameva darśayati tiṣṭhaditi svayamavikriyameva sadityarthaḥ /` — ‘That outstrips the running others,’ those unlike the Self—mind, speech, senses, and the like—who go swiftly; it goes beyond, as it were. The word ‘standing’ itself shows the sense ‘as it were’: the meaning is that it itself remains changeless.

**J7. tasmin is the Self-principle as the ever-conscious basis; mātariśvā is Vāyu/prāṇa; apaḥ means actions or activities, not waters; dadhāti is primarily ‘apportions/distributes,’ with ‘sustains’ as an allowed alternative.**
- chosen: Translate: ‘In that, Mātariśvan apportions the actions,’ understanding Mātariśvan as Vāyu/prāṇa and apaḥ as activities. (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- isup [13]: `tasminnātmatattve sati nityacaitanyasvabhāve mātariśvā vāyuḥ sarvaprāṇabhṛt kriyātmako` — In that Self-principle, whose nature is eternal consciousness, Mātariśvan is Vāyu, the bearer of all prāṇas, of the nature of activity.
- isup [13]: `apaḥ karmāṇi prāṇināṃ ceṣṭālakṣaṇāni, agnyādityaparjanyādīnāṃ jvalanadahanaprakāśābhivarṣaṇādilakṣaṇāni dadhāti vibhajati ityarthaḥ /` — ‘apaḥ’ means actions: the motions of living beings and the burning, consuming, shining, raining, and similar functions of fire, sun, rain-cloud, and so on. ‘dadhāti’ means ‘apportions/distributes.’
- isup [14]: `dhārayatīti vā` — Or it may mean ‘sustains.’
- isup [17]: `sarvā hi kāryakaraṇādivikriyā nityacaitanyātmasvarūpe sarvāspadabhūte satyeva bhavantītyarthaḥ` — All transformations of effects, instruments, and so forth occur only when the Self whose nature is eternal consciousness exists as the basis of all.

## Translation

> Unmoving, one, swifter than the mind—
the deva-s, the shining senses, did not reach this,
already gone before.

That, standing still, outstrips as it were
the others as they run.
In that Self-principle, Mātariśvan, the wind/prāṇa,
apportions the actions (apaḥ).

## One-shot delta

- A commentary-blind translation would likely render devāḥ simply as ‘gods’; Śaṅkara instead identifies them with the illuminating sense-faculties such as the eye.
- It would likely translate apaḥ as ‘waters’; Śaṅkara explicitly glosses it as karmāṇi, the activities/functions of living beings and cosmic powers.
- It might take the verse as asserting real motion of the Self; Śaṅkara repeatedly insists that the motion is only apparent through upādhi, while the Self is svayam avikriya, changeless in itself.
- It might miss that manasaḥ is ablative of comparison and that ‘mind’ means the saṅkalpa-vikalpa-type antaḥkaraṇa.
