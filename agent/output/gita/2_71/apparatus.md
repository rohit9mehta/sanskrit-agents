## Justifications

**J1. The absolutive is not taken as “having gone apart” but as vi-√hā in the sense “relinquishing.” sarvān is construed with kāmān and strengthened as complete, exhaustive abandonment.**
- chosen: vihāya = “having abandoned/renounced,” and sarvān qualifies kāmān as total abandonment. (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [1]: `vihāya parityajya kāmān yaḥ saṃnyāsī pumān sarvān aśeṣataḥ kārtsnyena carati` — ‘vihāya’ means ‘having cast aside’; the renunciant man abandons desires, all of them, without remainder, in their entirety.

**J2. The subject is the renunciant man, and carati is interpreted as paryaṭati, not as a general ethical “behaves.” The commentary adds that only the activity needed for bare life remains.**
- chosen: pumān carati = “the man moves about/wanders,” with only life-maintaining activity left. (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [1]: `yaḥ saṃnyāsī pumān ... carati, jīvana-mātra-ceṣṭāśeṣaḥ paryaṭatīty arthaḥ` — The man is a renunciant; ‘carati’ means ‘wanders,’ with only the activity for mere living left.

**J3. The term is not just generic desirelessness; Śaṅkara specifies that longing has departed even with respect to the mere maintenance of body and life.**
- chosen: niḥspṛhaḥ = free from longing even for mere bodily life. (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [2]: `niḥspṛhaḥ śarīra-jīvana-mātre 'pi nirgatā spṛhā yasya saḥ niḥspṛhaḥ san` — ‘niḥspṛha’ is he whose longing has gone away even regarding mere bodily life.

**J4. The translation avoids a merely modern “unselfish”; the commentary defines the word through absence of mamedam-abhiniveśa, possessive insistence, even in relation to requisites forced by bodily survival.**
- chosen: nirmamaḥ = free from the fixation ‘this is mine,’ even toward minimal life-imposed possessions. (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [2]: `nirmamaḥ śarīra-jīvana-mātrākṣipta-parigrahe 'pi mamedam ity abhiniveśa-varjitaḥ` — ‘nirmama’ means devoid of the fixation ‘this is mine,’ even regarding possessions taken up merely because of bodily life.

**J5. The commentary gives a precise target: not metaphysical individuality in general, but self-regard or self-estimation caused by learning and the like.**
- chosen: nirahaṃkāraḥ = devoid of self-conceit/ego-making based on such causes as being learned. (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [2]: `nirahaṃkāraḥ vidyāvattvādi-nimittātma-saṃbhāvanā-rahitaḥ ity etat` — ‘nirahaṃkāra’ means devoid of self-estimation on account of being learned and the like.

**J6. The object śānti is not mere calmness. Śaṅkara defines it as the nirvāṇa called cessation of all suffering of saṃsāra, and glosses adhigacchati by prāpnoti, ‘attains.’**
- chosen: śāntim adhigacchati = attains peace, namely nirvāṇa, the cessation of all saṃsāra-suffering; becomes Brahman. (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [3]: `śāntiṃ sarva-saṃsāra-duḥkhoparama-lakṣaṇāṃ nirvāṇākhyām adhigacchati prāpnoti brahma-bhūto bhavati ity arthaḥ` — He attains peace, called nirvāṇa, characterized by the cessation of all suffering of saṃsāra; ‘adhigacchati’ means ‘obtains,’ that is, becomes Brahman.

## Translation

> Having abandoned all desires, the man who wanders free from longing—
with only the activity needed for bare life remaining—
free from mine-making, free from ego-making:
he attains peace (śānti), the nirvāṇa that is the cessation of all suffering in saṃsāra.

## One-shot delta

- A commentary-blind translation might render carati vaguely as “acts” or “lives”; Śaṅkara specifically takes it as wandering with only life-maintaining activity left.
- It might flatten niḥspṛhaḥ, nirmamaḥ, and nirahaṃkāraḥ into generic “desireless, unselfish, egoless”; the bhāṣya gives more technical limits: no longing even for bodily life, no ‘mine’ fixation even toward minimal possessions, and no self-conceit based on learning and the like.
- It might take śānti as ordinary peace of mind; Śaṅkara identifies it with nirvāṇa, the cessation of all saṃsāra-suffering, and with becoming Brahman.
