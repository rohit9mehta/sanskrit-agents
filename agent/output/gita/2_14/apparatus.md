## Justifications

**J1. I translate mātrā-sparśāḥ as ‘contacts of the senses,’ taking mātrā as the sense organs and sparśa as their contact with sound and the other objects. Śaṅkara offers an alternative with athavā—‘mātrāḥ and sparśāḥ’ as senses and objects—but the primary gloss is the genitive tatpuruṣa mātrāṇāṃ sparśāḥ.**
- chosen: mātrā-sparśāḥ = contacts of the senses (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [1, 2]: `mātrā ābhiḥ mīyante śabdādaya iti śrotrādīni indriyāṇi |
mātrāṇāṃ sparśāḥ śabdādibhiḥ saṃyogāḥ |` — ‘Mātrās’ are the sense organs such as hearing, because sound and the rest are measured by them. ‘Contacts of the mātrās’ are conjunctions with sound and the like.
- gita [4, 5]: `athavā spṛśyanta iti sparśā viṣayāḥ śabdādayaḥ |
mātrāś ca sparśāś ca śītoṣṇa-sukha-duḥkha-dāḥ |` — Alternatively, ‘sparśas’ are objects such as sound, because they are touched; thus the mātrās and the sparśas give cold, heat, pleasure, and pain.

**J2. The compound is not merely a list of qualities but an adjective meaning that the sense-contacts produce or bestow these experiences. Śaṅkara also explains why cold/heat are named separately from pleasure/pain: cold and heat may sometimes be pleasant and sometimes painful, whereas pleasure and pain as such have fixed forms.**
- chosen: śītoṣṇa-sukha-duḥkha-dāḥ = giving cold and heat, pleasure and pain (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [3]: `te śītoṣṇasukha-duḥkha-dāḥ śītam uṣṇaṃ sukhaṃ duḥkhaṃ ca prayacchantīti |` — They are ‘givers of cold, heat, pleasure, and pain’: that is, they grant cold, heat, pleasure, and pain.
- gita [6, 7, 8, 9]: `śītaṃ kadācit sukhaṃ kadācit duḥkham |
tathā uṣṇam api aniyata-svarūpam |
sukha-duḥkhe punaḥ niyata-rūpe yato na vyabhicarataḥ |
atas tābhyāṃ pṛthak śītoṣṇayoḥ grahaṇam |` — Cold is sometimes pleasure and sometimes pain; likewise heat too has no fixed form. Pleasure and pain, however, have fixed forms, since they do not deviate. Therefore cold and heat are mentioned separately from those two.

**J3. I render āgamāpāyinaḥ as ‘coming and passing away’ rather than as a vague ‘arriving/departing’; Śaṅkara explicitly glosses it as āgamāpāyaśīlāḥ and states the causal point that because these are of such a nature, they are impermanent.**
- chosen: āgamāpāyinaḥ anityāḥ = of coming-and-going nature, hence impermanent (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [10]: `yasmāt te mātrā-sparśādayaḥ āgamāpāyinaḥ āgamāpāyaśīlāḥ tasmāt anityāḥ |` — Because these sense-contacts and the like are āgamāpāyinaḥ, having the nature of coming and passing away, therefore they are impermanent.

**J4. The accusative tān is construed with titikṣasva as the things to be endured, specified by Śaṅkara as śītoṣṇādīn, ‘cold and heat and the rest.’ Titikṣasva is glossed prasahasva, ‘bear/endure,’ with the practical sense of not producing elation or dejection toward them.**
- chosen: tān titikṣasva = endure those, such as cold and heat (depends on commentary: **yes**)
- gita [11, 12]: `ataḥ tān śītoṣṇādīn titikṣasva prasahasva |
teṣu harṣaṃ viṣādaṃ vā mā kārṣīḥ ity arthaḥ ||bhgs_2.14||` — Therefore endure—bear—those such as cold and heat. The meaning is: do not create elation or dejection with regard to them.

**J5. The commentary does not gloss tu; I render it as a light contrastive ‘but,’ fitting its normal discourse use here.**
- chosen: tu = But (depends on commentary: no)

## Translation

> But the contacts of the senses (mātrā-sparśāḥ), O son of Kuntī,
give cold and heat, pleasure and pain;
coming and passing away, they are impermanent—
endure them, O descendant of Bharata.

## Analyzer disagreements

- ByT5 gives titikṣ as the verbal lemma; because the requested format asks for the bare root, I record root/lemma as tij and note the desiderative stem titikṣa-.

## One-shot delta

- A commentary-blind translation might take mātrā in its common sense ‘measure’ and miss Śaṅkara’s identification of mātrās with the sense organs.
- It might miss Śaṅkara’s alternative analysis of mātrā-sparśāḥ as ‘senses and objects,’ even though the primary translation follows ‘contacts of the senses.’
- It might flatten śītoṣṇa-sukha-duḥkha-dāḥ into ‘cold-hot pleasant-painful’ rather than Śaṅkara’s ‘giving cold, heat, pleasure, and pain.’
- It might translate titikṣasva too literally as ‘desire to endure’; Śaṅkara’s gloss prasahasva supports the idiomatic imperative ‘endure/bear.’
- It might not see that Śaṅkara construes the intended discipline as not making harṣa or viṣāda—elation or dejection—toward these experiences.
