Triṃśikā (Vasubandhu) with Sthiramati's bhāṣya · 13

śāṭhyaṃ mado 'vihiṃsāhrīr atrapā styānam uddhataḥ āśraddhyam atha kausīdyaṃ pramādo muṣitā smṛtiḥ

Translation. Deceitful crookedness (śāṭhya), self-intoxicated pride (mada), harmfulness (vihiṃsā); shamelessness in oneself (ahrī/āhrīkya), no compunction before others (atrapā/anapatrāpya), torpor (styāna), restlessness (auddhatya); then faithlessness (āśraddhya), sloth (kausīdya), heedlessness (pramāda), and corrupted mindfulness—afflicted mindfulness (muṣitā smṛtiḥ).

How the commentary settled it

Where the verse alone is ambiguous, the reading follows Sthiramati's commentary; the deciding lines are quoted below each decision.
Translate śāṭhya not as generic ‘wickedness’ but as a technical mental crookedness that functions by bewildering others so as to conceal one’s own fault.
→ śāṭhya = deceitful crookedness of mind used to hide one’s own faults
trbh [841, 842, 843, 844, 845]śāṭhyaṃ svadoṣapracchādanopāyasaṃgṛhītañ cetasaḥ kauṭilyam svadoṣapracchādanopāyaḥ paravyāmohanam tat punar anyenānyat pratisaran vikṣipaty aparisphuṭaṃ vā pratipadyate ata eva śāṭhyaṃ mrakṣād bhidyate
Deceit is the crookedness of mind comprised in a means for concealing one’s own fault; that means is the bewildering of another, by shifting from one thing to another, distracting, or responding unclearly; this is why śāṭhya differs from mrakṣa.
trbh [849, 850, 851, 852, 853]lābhasatkārādhyavasitopāyābhyāṃ rāgamohābhyāṃ svadoṣapracchādanārthaṃ paravyāmohanāya pravartate tayor eva sahitayoḥ prajñapyate idañ ca samyagavavādalābhaparipanthakarmakam
It operates, through attachment and delusion directed to gain and honor as means, for hiding one’s own fault and bewildering another; it is posited on those two together and obstructs the obtaining of correct instruction.
Render mada as intoxicated pride/elation, not merely ‘intoxication’ or ‘rapture’, because the commentary defines its object and mental effect.
→ mada = self-intoxicated elation that takes over the mind
trbh [856, 857, 858, 859, 860, 861, 862]madaḥ svasaṃpattau raktasyoddharṣaś cetasaḥ paryādānam kulārogyayauvanabalarūpaiśvaryabuddhimedhāprakarṣaḥ svasaṃpattiḥ uddharṣo harṣaviśeṣaḥ yena harṣaviśeṣeṇa cittam asvatantrīkriyate tena tadātmatantrīkaraṇāt paryāttaṃ bhavatīty etad uktañ cetasaḥ paryādānam iti ayañ ca sarvakleśopakleśasaṃniśrayadānakarmakaḥ
Mada is, in one attached to one’s own excellences, an elation, a taking-over of the mind. One’s excellences are superiority in family, health, youth, strength, beauty, power, intelligence, or wisdom. The elation is a special joy by which the mind is made unfree, hence ‘taking-over of the mind’; it provides support for all afflictions and secondary afflictions.
Despite the supplied apostrophe before vihiṃsā, the commentary compels vihiṃsā ‘harming’, not avihiṃsā ‘non-harming’.
→ vihiṃsā = harmfulness/cruelty toward beings
trbh [863, 864, 865, 866, 867, 868, 869]vihiṃsā sattvaviheṭhanā vividhair vadhabandhanatāḍanatarjanādibhiḥ sattvānāṃ hiṃsā vihiṃsā viheṭhyante 'nayā sattvā vadhabandhanādibhir duḥkhadaurmanasyotpādanād iti sattvaviheṭhanā sā punaḥ pratighāṃśikī nirghṛṇatā sattveṣu cittarūkṣatā sattvaviheṭhanakarmikā vihiṃsety ucyate
Vihiṃsā is the injuring of beings: harming beings by various acts such as killing, binding, beating, and threatening. Beings are harassed by it through killing and binding and by the production of suffering and dejection. It is anger-related; mercilessness, harshness of mind toward beings, functioning to harass beings, is called vihiṃsā.
Do not translate hrī/trapā as positive ‘shame’ or ‘bashfulness’; the verse has privative forms corresponding to āhrīkya and anapatrāpya, distinguished as self-regarding and other-regarding lack of shame.
→ ahrī and atrapā = the two shamelessnesses, āhrīkya and anapatrāpya
trbh [870, 871, 872]āhrīkyaṃ svayam avadyenālajjā tasmin karmaṇyātmānam ayogyaṃ manyamānasyāpi yāvadyenālajjā sāhrīkyaṃ hrīvipakṣabhūtam
Āhrīkya is not being ashamed by oneself regarding what is blameworthy; even when one regards oneself as unfit for that action, the lack of shame regarding the blameworthy is āhrīkya, the opposite of hrī.
trbh [873, 874, 875, 876]anapatrāpyaṃ parato 'vadyenālajjā lokaśāstraviruddham etan mayā kriyata ity evam avagacchato 'pi yā tayā pāpakriyayālajjā sāpatrāpyavipakṣabhūtam anapatrāpyam
Anapatrāpya is not being ashamed before others regarding what is blameworthy: even while recognizing ‘this done by me is opposed to the world and the śāstra,’ lack of shame about that evil action is anapatrāpya, the opposite of apatrāpya.
Translate styāna as a mental factor of unfitness and dull stiffness, not merely as the adjective ‘dense’ or ‘coagulated’.
→ styāna = torpor, mental unworkability/stiffness
trbh [881, 882, 883, 884, 885, 886, 887]styānañ cittasyākarmaṇyatā staimityam stimitasya bhāvaḥ staimityaṃ yadyogāc cittaṃ jaḍībhavati stimitaṃ bhavati nālambanaṃ pratipattuṃ samutsahate etac ca sarvakleśopakleśasahāyyadānakarmakam mohāṃśe prajñaptatvāc ca mohāṃśikam eva na pṛthag vidyate
Styāna is the mind’s unfitness for work, its stiffness. Stiffness is that through whose association the mind becomes dull and inert and does not make effort to apprehend an object. It assists all afflictions and secondary afflictions; since it is posited in a portion of delusion, it is delusion-related and not separately existent.
Although the supplied verse surface is uddhataḥ, Sthiramati comments on auddhatyaṃ; therefore the translation gives the technical mental factor ‘restlessness/non-quiescence’, not an ‘arrogant/uplifted man’.
→ uddhataḥ in the verse represents auddhatya = mental non-tranquillity/restlessness
trbh [888, 889, 890, 891]auddhatyaṃ cittasyāvyupaśamaḥ vyupaśamo hi śamathas tadviruddho 'vyupaśamaḥ sa punar eṣa rāgānukūlaṃ pūrvahasitarasitakrīḍitādyanusmarataś cetaso 'vyupaśamahetuḥ śamathaparipanthakarmakaḥ
Auddhatya is the mind’s non-tranquillity. Tranquillity is calm, and non-tranquillity is its opposite. It arises when the mind recalls former laughter, talk, play, and the like in a way favorable to attachment; it causes non-tranquillity and obstructs calm.
Read and translate the bhāṣya-supported āśraddhyam as an abstract fault opposed to śraddhā, not the vulgate āśraddham or a vague ‘unbelief’.
→ āśraddhya = lack of conviction/faith in karma, fruit, truths, and jewels
trbh [892, 893, 894, 895, 896, 897, 898, 899]āśraddhyāṃ karmaphalasatyaratneṣv anabhisaṃpratyayaḥ śraddhāvipakṣaḥ śraddhā hy astitvaguṇavattvaśakyatveṣv abhisaṃpratyayaḥ prasādo 'bhilāṣaś ca yathākramam aśraddhā tadviparyeṇāstitvaguṇavattvaśakyatveṣv anabhisaṃpratyayo 'prasādo 'nabhilāṣaś ca kausīdyasaṃniśrayadānakarmakam aśraddadhānasya prayogacchandābhāvāt kausīdyasaṃniśraya-dānakarmakatvam
Faithlessness is lack of conviction regarding karma, its fruit, the truths, and the jewels; it is the opposite of faith. Faith is conviction, clarity, and aspiration regarding existence, possession of qualities, and attainability respectively. Non-faith is the contrary: lack of conviction, lack of clarity, and lack of aspiration, and it gives support to sloth because one without faith lacks desire for practice.
Translate kausīdya specifically as the mind’s failure to exert itself in wholesome action and as the opposite of vigor.
→ kausīdya = sloth, lack of effort in the wholesome
trbh [900, 901, 902, 903, 904]kausīdyaṃ kuśale cetaso 'nabhyutsāho vīryavipakṣaḥ kuśale kāyavāṅmanaḥkarmaṇi nidrāpārśvaśayanasukham āgamya yo mohāṃśikaś cetaso 'nabhyutsāhaḥ etac ca kuśalapakṣaprayogaparipanthakarmakam
Kausīdya is the mind’s non-exertion in the wholesome, the opposite of vigor: delusion-related lack of effort in wholesome bodily, verbal, and mental action through resorting to the pleasure of sleep and lying on one’s side. It obstructs undertaking the wholesome side.
Do not render pramāda here as intoxication or madness; Sthiramati defines it as a constructed fault based on greed, hatred, delusion, and sloth, with ethical consequences.
→ pramāda = heedlessness, failure to guard the mind and cultivate the wholesome
trbh [905, 906, 907, 908, 909, 910]pramādo yair lobhadveṣamohakausīdyaiḥ kleśād rāgadveṣamohādikāc cittaṃ na rakṣati kaśalaṃ ca tatpratipakṣabhūtaṃ na bhāvayati teṣu lobhadveṣamohakausīdyeṣu pramādaḥ prajñapyate ayañ cākuśalavṛddhikuśalaparihāṇisaṃniśrayadānakarmakaḥ
Pramāda is that by which, through greed, hatred, delusion, and sloth—through affliction beginning with attachment, hatred, and delusion—one does not guard the mind and does not cultivate the wholesome that is its antidote. It is posited on greed, hatred, delusion, and sloth and gives support to the increase of the unwholesome and the decline of the wholesome.
Construe muṣitā as an adjective modifying smṛtiḥ and translate the phrase by the commentator’s equation with kliṣṭā smṛtiḥ, not literally as ‘stolen memory’.
→ muṣitā smṛtiḥ = corrupted/afflicted mindfulness
trbh [911, 912, 913, 914]muṣitā smṛtiḥ kliṣṭā smṛtiḥ kliṣṭeti kleśasaṃprayuktā iyañ ca vikṣepasaṃniśrayadānakarmikā
Muṣitā smṛtiḥ is afflicted mindfulness; ‘afflicted’ means associated with affliction. It functions to give support to distraction.
