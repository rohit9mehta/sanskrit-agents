Triṃśikā (Vasubandhu) with Sthiramati's bhāṣya · 10

adyāḥ sparśādayaḥ chandādhimokṣasmṛtayaḥ saha | samādhidhībhyāṃ niyatāḥ śraddhātha hrīr apatrapā

Translation. The initial ones (ādyāḥ)—those beginning with contact (sparśa)—are the omnipresent (sarvatraga); desire (chanda), resolve (adhimokṣa), and mindfulness (smṛti), together with concentration (samādhi) and discernment (dhī/prajñā), are the object-specific determinate factors (niyata/viniyata); then come faith (śraddhā), shame (hrī), and compunction (apatrapā).

How the commentary settled it

Where the verse alone is ambiguous, the reading follows Sthiramati's commentary; the deciding lines are quoted below each decision.
Read and translate the opening as ‘the initial ones … are the omnipresent,’ following the commentary’s explicit gloss.
→ ādyāḥ means the already-first-mentioned omnipresent factors (sarvatraga), not an unlengthened adyāḥ from ad/adya.
trbh [496]ādau nirdiṣṭatvād ādyāḥ sarvatragā ity arthaḥ
Because they were indicated at the beginning, they are ‘initial’; the meaning is ‘omnipresent.’
trbh [498, 499]sadāsparśamanaskāravitsaṃjñācetanānvitam iti prathamato nirdiṣṭāḥ
The earlier quoted phrase shows that they were indicated first.
Analyze the compound as bahuvrīhi and translate ‘those beginning with contact,’ not merely ‘contact and so on’ without its category function.
→ sparśādayaḥ is a bahuvrīhi: ‘those whose first is contact,’ i.e. the five omnipresent factors beginning with sparśa.
trbh [500]sparśa eṣām ādir iti sparśādayaḥ
Contact is their beginning; therefore they are ‘those beginning with contact.’
trbh [501, 502]te punaḥ sparśamanaskārādayaḥ pañca dharmāḥ sarvacittam anugacchantīti sarvatragāḥ
Those, again, are the five dharmas beginning with contact and attention; because they accompany every mind, they are omnipresent.
trbh [503, 504]tathā hy eta ālayavijñāne kliṣṭe manasi pravṛttivijñāneṣu cāviśeṣeṇa pravartante
For these occur without distinction in ālayavijñāna, in the afflicted mind, and in the active consciousnesses.
Treat chandādhimokṣasmṛtayaḥ as a three-member dvandva and samādhidhībhyām as a two-member instrumental dual dvandva joined by saha.
→ chandādhimokṣasmṛtayaḥ saha samādhidhībhyām enumerates five distinct viniyata dharmas; saha governs the instrumental dual and does not assert that they always co-occur.
trbh [505, 506, 507]viniyatān adhikṛtyāha chandādhimokṣasmṛtayaḥ saha samādhidhībhyāṃ niyatāḥ
With regard to the determinate factors he says: desire, resolve, and mindfulness, together with samādhi and dhī, are niyata.
trbh [568, 569, 570, 571]ete hi pañca dharmāḥ parasparaṃ vyatiricyāpi pravartante evañ ca yatrādhimokṣas tatra nāvaśyam itarair api bhavitavyam evaṃ sarvatra vācyam
For these five dharmas occur even while distinct from one another. Thus, where adhimokṣa is present, the others need not necessarily also be present; this should be said in every case.
Translate niyatāḥ as ‘object-specific determinate factors’ because the commentary defines them by restriction to a special object, not by universal occurrence.
→ niyatāḥ is the technical class viniyatāḥ, ‘determinate/object-specific,’ not ‘necessary’ or merely ‘restrained.’
trbh [508, 509]viśeṣe niyatatvād viniyatāḥ eṣāṃ hi viśeṣa eva viṣayo na sarvaḥ
They are viniyata because they are determined with respect to a specific case; for their object is precisely a specific one, not every object.
trbh [572]uktā viniyatāḥ
The determinate factors have been stated.
Use technical English glosses constrained by the commentary rather than dictionary defaults such as ‘meter’ for chanda, ‘tradition’ for smṛti, or ‘meditation’ alone for samādhi.
→ Translate the five viniyata terms according to Sthiramati’s definitions: chanda as desire/aspiration, adhimokṣa as resolve/ascertainment, smṛti as mindfulness/recollection, samādhi as concentration, and dhī as discernment/prajñā.
trbh [510]tatra chando 'bhiprete vastuny abhilāṣaḥ
Here chanda is desire for an intended object.
trbh [515]darśanaśravaṇādiprārthanā chandaḥ
Chanda is the aspiration for seeing, hearing, and the like.
trbh [517]adhimokṣo niścite vastuni tathaivāvadhāraṇam
Adhimokṣa is ascertainment just so with respect to a settled object.
trbh [524, 525]evam etan nānyathety avadhāraṇam adhimokṣaḥ
The ascertainment ‘this is so, not otherwise’ is adhimokṣa.
trbh [529]smṛtiḥ saṃstute vastuny asaṃpramoṣaś cetaso 'bhilapanatā
Smṛti is non-loss with respect to a familiar object, the mind’s verbal noting.
trbh [533, 534]pūrvagṛhītasya vastunaḥ punaḥ punar ālambanākārasmaraṇam abhilapanatā abhilapanam evābhilapanatā
Verbal noting is repeatedly remembering the object-aspect of a previously apprehended thing; verbal noting itself is verbal-noting-ness.
trbh [538, 539, 540, 541]samādhir upaparīkṣye vastuni cittasyaikāgratā upaparīkṣyaṃ vastu guṇato doṣato vā ekāgratā ekālambanatā
Samādhi is the one-pointedness of mind on an object to be examined, whether by its qualities or defects; one-pointedness is having one object.
trbh [545, 546, 547, 548, 549, 550]dhīḥ prajñā sāpy upaparīkṣya eva vastuni pravicayo yogāyogavihito 'nyathā veti pravicinotīti pravicayaḥ yaḥ samyaṅ mithyā vā saṃkīrṇasvasāmānyalakṣaṇeṣv iva dharmeṣu vivekāvabodhaḥ
Dhī is prajñā. It too, with respect to an object to be examined, is analysis produced by correct method, by incorrect method, or otherwise. Analysis is so called because one analyzes; whether correct or mistaken, it is discriminative understanding of dharmas with mixed own- and common-characteristics.
Do not include śraddhā, hrī, and apatrapā among the five niyata factors; translate them as the next, wholesome, items.
→ śraddhā atha hrīḥ apatrapā starts the kuśala group; atha marks transition after the viniyata factors.
trbh [572, 573, 574]uktā viniyatāḥ tadanantaroddiṣṭās tv idānīṃ kuśalā vaktavyā ity ata āha śraddhātha hrīr apatrapā
The determinate factors have been stated. Now the wholesome factors indicated immediately after them are to be stated; therefore he says: ‘faith, then shame, apatrapā.’
