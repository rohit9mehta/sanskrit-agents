Triṃśikā (Vasubandhu) with Sthiramati's bhāṣya · 20

yena yena vikalpena yad yad vastu vikalpyate | parikalpita evāsau svabhāvo na sa vidyate

Translation. By whatever conceptual construction (vikalpa), whatever object is construed, that nature (svabhāva) is just the imagined (parikalpita); it is not found to exist.

How the commentary settled it

Where the verse alone is ambiguous, the reading follows Sthiramati's commentary; the deciding lines are quoted below each decision.
The verse is not about ‘options’ in the ordinary sense but about the endless plurality of vikalpas according to the difference of their construed objects, internal or external, even up to Buddha-dharmas.
→ ‘conceptual construction’ for vikalpa; ‘is construed/imagined’ for vikalpyate; the repeated yena yena / yad yad is distributive over an unlimited range of imagined objects.
trbh [1297, 1298, 1299, 1300]ādhyātmikabāhyavikalpyavastubhedena vikalpānām ānantyaṃ pradarśayann āha yena yena vikalpeneti yad yad vastu prakalpyate ādhyātmikaṃ bāhyaṃ vāntaśo yāvad buddhadharmā api
He says this while showing the infinitude of constructions through the difference of internal and external objects to be construed: ‘by whatever construction’; whatever object is projected/construed—internal or external, even as far as Buddha-dharmas.
Although neuter nominative and accusative are formally identical, the passive verb and Sthiramati’s restatement with prakalpyate make vastu the thing undergoing conceptual construction, not the object of an unexpressed active agent.
→ yad yad vastu is construed as the neuter nominative subject of the passive vikalpyate: ‘whatever object is construed’.
trbh [1299, 1304]yad yad vastu prakalpyate yad vastu vikalpyaviṣayas tad yasmāt sattābhāvān na vidyate
Whatever object is projected/construed; whatever object is the domain/object-to-be-construed, since, because of absence of being, it does not exist.
Sthiramati explicitly restates the verse as parikalpita-svabhāva and contrasts it with a hetu-pratyaya-pratibaddha-svabhāva, a nature bound to causes and conditions.
→ parikalpita evāsau svabhāvaḥ means ‘that nature is just the imagined (parikalpita) nature’, not a causally conditioned own-being.
trbh [1301, 1305, 1306, 1307]parikalpita evāsau svabhāva iti tasmāt tad vastu parikalpitasvabhāvam eva na hetupratyayapratibaddhasvabhāvam
‘That nature is just the imagined,’ he says. Therefore that object has only an imagined nature, not a nature bound to causes and conditions.
trbh [1313]sarvam idaṃ vikalpamātram eva tadarthasya parikalpitarūpatvāt
All this is merely construction, because its referent has the form of what is imagined.
Sthiramati introduces this as the reason and explains the negation by sattā-abhāva, the absence of being. The sūtra quotation also uses vidyante existentially: dharmas do not exist in the way childish ordinary people grasp them.
→ na sa vidyate is existential: ‘it is not found / does not exist’, not merely ‘it is not known’.
trbh [1302, 1303, 1304]atra kāraṇam āha na sa vidyata iti yad vastu vikalpyaviṣayas tad yasmāt sattābhāvān na vidyate
Here he states the reason: ‘it is not found/does not exist.’ Whatever object is the domain of construction, since, because of absence of being, it does not exist.
trbh [1315]na khalu punaḥ subhūte dharmās tathā vidyante yathā bālapṛthagjanā abhiniviṣṭā
For, Subhūti, dharmas do not exist in the way that childish ordinary people are attached to them.
Sthiramati argues from the observed arising of many mutually contradictory constructions with respect to one object or its absence, and rejects the possibility that one object or absence has many mutually contradictory natures.
→ The translation avoids implying a real substrate possessing many real contradictory natures; the referent is imagined, and all this is vikalpa-mātra.
trbh [1308, 1309, 1310, 1311, 1312, 1313]tathā hy ekasmin vastuni tadabhāve ca parasparaviruddhānekavikalpapravṛttir dṛṣṭā na ca tad ekaṃ vastu tadabhāvo vā parasparaviruddhānekasvabhāvo yujyate tasmāt sarvam idaṃ vikalpamātram eva tadarthasya parikalpitarūpatvāt
For thus, with respect to one object and also its absence, the occurrence of many mutually contradictory constructions is observed. And it is not tenable that that one object, or its absence, have many mutually contradictory natures. Therefore all this is merely construction, because its referent has an imagined form.
