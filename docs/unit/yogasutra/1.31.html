30 duḥkhadaurmanasyāṅgamejayatvaśvāsapraśvāsā vikṣepasahabhuvaḥ

How the commentary settled it

Where the verse alone is ambiguous, the reading follows Vyāsa's commentary; the deciding lines are quoted below each decision.
The first long word is not a single unanalyzed abstract noun but a coordinated list: suffering, despondency, limb-trembling, in-breath, and out-breath. Vyāsa defines each item in order and then resumes them collectively with ete.
→ duḥkha–daurmanasya–aṅgamejayatva–śvāsa–praśvāsāḥ as a five-item dvandva subject
ys [1, 2]duḥkham ādhyātmikam ādhibhautikam ādhidaivikaṃ ca. yenābhihatāḥ prāṇinas tadapaghātāya prayatante tad duḥkham.
Suffering is internal, caused by beings, and divine. That by which living beings, when struck, strive for its removal—that is suffering.
ys [3, 4, 5, 6]daurmanasyam icchāvighātāc cetasaḥ kṣobhaḥ. yad aṅgāny ejayati kampayati tad aṅgamejayatvam. prāṇo yad bāhyaṃ vāyum ācāmati sa śvāsaḥ. yat kauṣṭhyaṃ vāyuṃ niḥsārayati sa praśvāsaḥ.
Despondency is disturbance of the mind from the thwarting of desire. What shakes and trembles the limbs is limb-trembling. When prāṇa draws in the external air, that is śvāsa. When it sends out the abdominal air, that is praśvāsa.
ys [7]ete vikṣepasahabhuvo vikṣiptacittasyaite bhavanti.
These are concomitant with distraction; these occur in one whose mind is distracted.
The translation uses “suffering,” since Vyāsa defines duḥkha as threefold affliction and as something beings try to remove when struck by it.
→ duḥkha = suffering/pain, not merely difficulty
ys [1, 2]duḥkham ādhyātmikam ādhibhautikam ādhidaivikaṃ ca. yenābhihatāḥ prāṇinas tadapaghātāya prayatante tad duḥkham.
Suffering is internal, caused by beings, and divine. That by which living beings, when struck, strive for its removal—that is suffering.
Rather than a vague “bad-mindedness,” daurmanasya is rendered as despondency because Vyāsa specifies mental agitation caused by obstruction of desire.
→ daurmanasya = despondency/mental disturbance from frustrated desire
ys [3]daurmanasyam icchāvighātāc cetasaḥ kṣobhaḥ.
Despondency is disturbance of the mind from the thwarting of desire.
The term is rendered concretely as trembling of the limbs/body, following Vyāsa’s causative gloss ejayati kampayati.
→ aṅgamejayatva = limb-trembling
ys [4]yad aṅgāny ejayati kampayati tad aṅgamejayatvam.
What shakes and trembles the limbs is limb-trembling.
Vyāsa fixes the direction of the two breath-terms: śvāsa is the drawing in of external air, and praśvāsa is the expelling of abdominal air. The translation therefore gives “in-breath and out-breath.”
→ śvāsa = in-breath; praśvāsa = out-breath
ys [5]prāṇo yad bāhyaṃ vāyum ācāmati sa śvāsaḥ.
When prāṇa draws in the external air, that is śvāsa.
ys [6]yat kauṣṭhyaṃ vāyuṃ niḥsārayati sa praśvāsaḥ.
When it sends out the abdominal air, that is praśvāsa.
The final compound is taken predicatively: these five phenomena co-occur with distraction, rather than being simply identical with the primary obstacles. Vyāsa says they occur for the vikṣipta-citta and not for the samāhita-citta. The printed/commentarial sandhi vikṣiptacittasyaite is read as vikṣiptacittasya ete.
→ vikṣepasahabhuvaḥ = concomitants of distraction, occurring in a distracted mind
ys [7]ete vikṣepasahabhuvo vikṣiptacittasyaite bhavanti.
These are concomitant with distraction; these occur in one whose mind is distracted.
ys [8]samāhitacittasyaite na bhavanti.
These do not occur in one whose mind is composed.
