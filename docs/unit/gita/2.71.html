Bhagavad-Gītā with Śaṅkara's bhāṣya · 2.71

vihāya kāmān yaḥ sarvān pumāṃś carati niḥspṛhaḥ | nirmamo nirahaṃkāraḥ sa śāntim adhigacchati

Translation. Having abandoned all desires, the man who wanders free from longing— with only the activity needed for bare life remaining— free from mine-making, free from ego-making: he attains peace (śānti), the nirvāṇa that is the cessation of all suffering in saṃsāra.

How the commentary settled it

Where the verse alone is ambiguous, the reading follows Śaṅkara's commentary; the deciding lines are quoted below each decision.
The absolutive is not taken as “having gone apart” but as vi-√hā in the sense “relinquishing.” sarvān is construed with kāmān and strengthened as complete, exhaustive abandonment.
→ vihāya = “having abandoned/renounced,” and sarvān qualifies kāmān as total abandonment.
gita [1]vihāya parityajya kāmān yaḥ saṃnyāsī pumān sarvān aśeṣataḥ kārtsnyena carati
‘vihāya’ means ‘having cast aside’; the renunciant man abandons desires, all of them, without remainder, in their entirety.
The subject is the renunciant man, and carati is interpreted as paryaṭati, not as a general ethical “behaves.” The commentary adds that only the activity needed for bare life remains.
→ pumān carati = “the man moves about/wanders,” with only life-maintaining activity left.
gita [1]yaḥ saṃnyāsī pumān ... carati, jīvana-mātra-ceṣṭāśeṣaḥ paryaṭatīty arthaḥ
The man is a renunciant; ‘carati’ means ‘wanders,’ with only the activity for mere living left.
The term is not just generic desirelessness; Śaṅkara specifies that longing has departed even with respect to the mere maintenance of body and life.
→ niḥspṛhaḥ = free from longing even for mere bodily life.
gita [2]niḥspṛhaḥ śarīra-jīvana-mātre 'pi nirgatā spṛhā yasya saḥ niḥspṛhaḥ san
‘niḥspṛha’ is he whose longing has gone away even regarding mere bodily life.
The translation avoids a merely modern “unselfish”; the commentary defines the word through absence of mamedam-abhiniveśa, possessive insistence, even in relation to requisites forced by bodily survival.
→ nirmamaḥ = free from the fixation ‘this is mine,’ even toward minimal life-imposed possessions.
gita [2]nirmamaḥ śarīra-jīvana-mātrākṣipta-parigrahe 'pi mamedam ity abhiniveśa-varjitaḥ
‘nirmama’ means devoid of the fixation ‘this is mine,’ even regarding possessions taken up merely because of bodily life.
The commentary gives a precise target: not metaphysical individuality in general, but self-regard or self-estimation caused by learning and the like.
→ nirahaṃkāraḥ = devoid of self-conceit/ego-making based on such causes as being learned.
gita [2]nirahaṃkāraḥ vidyāvattvādi-nimittātma-saṃbhāvanā-rahitaḥ ity etat
‘nirahaṃkāra’ means devoid of self-estimation on account of being learned and the like.
The object śānti is not mere calmness. Śaṅkara defines it as the nirvāṇa called cessation of all suffering of saṃsāra, and glosses adhigacchati by prāpnoti, ‘attains.’
→ śāntim adhigacchati = attains peace, namely nirvāṇa, the cessation of all saṃsāra-suffering; becomes Brahman.
gita [3]śāntiṃ sarva-saṃsāra-duḥkhoparama-lakṣaṇāṃ nirvāṇākhyām adhigacchati prāpnoti brahma-bhūto bhavati ity arthaḥ
He attains peace, called nirvāṇa, characterized by the cessation of all suffering of saṃsāra; ‘adhigacchati’ means ‘obtains,’ that is, becomes Brahman.
