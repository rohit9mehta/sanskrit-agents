Bhagavad-Gītā with Śaṅkara's bhāṣya · 2.25

avyakto 'yam acintyo 'yam avikāryo 'yam ucyate | tasmād evaṃ viditvainaṃ nānuśocitum arhasi

Translation. This Self is unmanifest (avyakta); this Self is inconceivable (acintya); this Self is said to be unmodifiable (avikārya). Therefore, having known this Self in this way, you ought not to grieve.

How the commentary settled it

Where the verse alone is ambiguous, the reading follows Śaṅkara's commentary; the deciding lines are quoted below each decision.
The repeated demonstrative ayam is translated as “this Self,” because Śaṅkara explicitly supplies ātmā with the predicates.
→ ayam throughout refers to the Self (ātmā), not to the body or to an unspecified person.
gita [1]avyaktaḥ sarva-karaṇāviṣayatvāt na vyajyata iti avyakto 'yam ātmā |
Because it is not an object of any instrument, it is not manifested; therefore this Self is avyakta.
gita [4]ayaṃ tv ātmā anindriya-gocaratvāt acintyaḥ |
But this Self, because it is not within the range of the senses, is acintya.
gita [8]avikriyatvāt avikāryo 'yam ātmā ucyate |
Because of being without modification, this Self is called avikārya.
The word is not taken as the Sāṃkhya technical noun ‘the Unmanifest’ or as merely ‘undeveloped’; Śaṅkara derives the sense from na vyajyate and sarva-karaṇāviṣayatva.
→ avyakta is “unmanifest/imperceptible,” in the sense of not being objectified by any instrument of cognition.
gita [1]avyaktaḥ sarva-karaṇāviṣayatvāt na vyajyata iti avyakto 'yam ātmā |
Avyakta: because it is not an object of any instrument; it is not manifested, hence this Self is avyakta.
The translation avoids “not to be thought about” as a prescription; Śaṅkara presents an epistemic reason for its being beyond thought.
→ acintya is “inconceivable/beyond thought,” because thought has sense-objects as its field and the Self is not a sense-object.
gita [2, 3, 4]ataeva acintyo 'yam | yad dhi indriya-gocaras tac cintā-viṣayatvam āpadyate | ayaṃ tv ātmā anindriya-gocaratvāt acintyaḥ |
Therefore this is acintya. Whatever is within the range of the senses becomes an object of thought; but this Self, because it is not within the range of the senses, is acintya.
The commentary ties avikārya to absence of vikāra, contrasting the Self with milk transformed into curd and then explaining it by niravayavatva and avikriyatva.
→ avikārya is “unmodifiable/unchangeable,” specifically because the Self is avikriya, not subject to transformation and without parts.
gita [5]ataeva avikāryaḥ, yathā kṣīraṃ dadhyātañcanādinā vikāri na tathā ayam ātmā |
Therefore it is avikārya: as milk is transformed by curdling agents, this Self is not so.
gita [6]niravayavatvāc cāvikriyaḥ |
And because it is without parts, it is without modification.
gita [8]avikriyatvāt avikāryo 'yam ātmā ucyate |
Because of being without modification, this Self is called avikārya.
The commentary fixes both the scope of evam and the referent of enam, and it construes the result as the impropriety of grieving over agency and slaughter.
→ tasmāt evaṃ viditvā enam means “therefore, having known this Self in this way,” with evam = yathokta-prakāreṇa and enam = ātmānam.
gita [9]tasmāt evaṃ yathokta-prakāreṇa enam ātmānaṃ viditvā tvaṃ na anuśocitum arhasi hantāham eṣām, mayaite hanyanta iti ||bhgs_2.25||
Therefore, having known this Self in this way, in the manner stated, you ought not to grieve, thinking ‘I am their slayer; by me these are slain.’
With an infinitive, arhasi has the modal sense of fitness/obligation; Śaṅkara repeats the construction directly with tvaṃ and explains the false basis of grief.
→ na anuśocitum arhasi is rendered “you ought not to grieve,” not merely “you cannot grieve.”
gita [9]tvaṃ na anuśocitum arhasi hantāham eṣām, mayaite hanyanta iti
You ought not to grieve, thinking ‘I am their slayer; by me these are slain.’
