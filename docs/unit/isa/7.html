How the commentary settled it

Where the verse alone is ambiguous, the reading follows Śaṅkara's commentary; the deciding lines are quoted below each decision.
The locative relative yasmin and the correlative tatra are not forced into a merely spatial reading. Śaṅkara glosses yasmin both as yasmin kāle and as yathoktātmani, and tatra correspondingly as tasmin kāle or tatrātmani. The translation therefore preserves both: ‘At the time when—or in the Self in which ... there.’
→ yasmin/tatra is rendered with Śaṅkara’s two permitted scopes: temporal ‘when/then’ and locative ‘in the Self/there.’
isup [1]yasminkāle yathoktātmani vā tānyeva bhūtāni sarvāṇi paramārthātmadarśanādātmaivābhūd ātmaiva saṃvṛtaḥ paramārthavastu vijānataḥ tatra tasminkāle tatrātmani vā ko mohaḥ kaḥ śokaḥ /
‘At the time when, or in the aforesaid Self ... there, at that time or in that Self, what delusion, what sorrow?’
The commentary makes the ‘becoming the Self alone’ depend on paramārthātmadarśana, vision of the ultimate Self. Thus ātmā eva is restrictive: all beings are known as nothing but the Self in true knowledge.
→ sarvāṇi bhūtāni are said to have become/been recognized as the Self alone for the knower, not as a physical transformation of beings into an individual ego.
isup [1]tānyeva bhūtāni sarvāṇi paramārthātmadarśanādātmaivābhūd
‘Those very beings, all of them, because of the vision of the ultimate Self, became the Self alone.’
The genitive participle is not a finite verb. Śaṅkara glosses its object as paramārthavastu, ‘the ultimate reality,’ and contrasts it with ajānataḥ, ‘of one who does not know,’ for whom sorrow and delusion occur.
→ vijānataḥ means ‘for/of the one who knows the highest reality.’
isup [1]paramārthavastu vijānataḥ
‘of the one knowing the ultimate reality’
isup [2]śokaśca mohaśca kāmakarmabījam ajānato bhavati /
‘Sorrow and delusion, the seed of desire and action, occur for one who does not know.’
The interrogatives are not requests for information. Śaṅkara explains that sorrow and delusion are effects of avidyā and that the verse shows their impossibility by rhetorical challenge; therefore the translation ‘what delusion, what sorrow?’ carries the force ‘none is possible.’
→ ko mohaḥ kaḥ śokaḥ is a rhetorical denial of the possibility of delusion and sorrow for the knower.
isup [2]śokaśca mohaśca kāmakarmabījam ajānato bhavati /
‘Sorrow and delusion, the seed of desire and action, occur for one who does not know.’
isup [3]na tvātmaikatvaṃ viśuddhaṃ gaganopamaṃ paśyataḥ ko mohaḥ kaḥ śoka iti śokamohayoravidyākāryayorākṣepeṇa asambhavapradarśanāt sakāraṇasya saṃsārasyātyantamevocchedaḥ pradarśito bhavati
‘But not for one who sees the pure, sky-like oneness of the Self: “what delusion, what sorrow?” By this challenge the impossibility of sorrow and delusion, effects of ignorance, is shown, and the absolute cutting off of saṃsāra together with its cause is displayed.’
The commentary glosses ekatvam as ātmaikatvaṃ and anupaśyataḥ by paśyataḥ. This excludes reading ekatvam as a separate eka + tvam sequence and excludes a merely numerical or social ‘unity’; Śaṅkara’s object is the pure oneness of the Self.
→ ekatvam anupaśyataḥ is ‘for one beholding Self-oneness,’ with ekatvam as the accusative object of the participle.
isup [3]na tvātmaikatvaṃ viśuddhaṃ gaganopamaṃ paśyataḥ
‘But not for one who sees the pure, sky-like oneness of the Self.’
This is decided on grammar rather than commentary: abhūt is the root-aorist form of √bhū, while the regular imperfect would be abhavat. The commentary supports the meaning ‘became/was,’ but the lakāra identification is grammatical.
→ abhūt is analyzed as Luṅ/aorist 3rd singular of √bhū.
