Īśopaniṣad with Śaṅkara's bhāṣya · 4

anejad ekaṃ manaso javīyo nainaddevā āpnuvanpūrvamarṣat | taddhāvato 'nyānatyeti tiṣṭhat tasminn apo mātariśvā dadhāti

Translation. Unmoving, one, swifter than the mind— the senses, the “shining ones” (devāḥ), did not reach this, already gone before. Standing still, it outstrips the other runners. In that self-principle, Mātariśvan, the Wind, allots the actions.

How the commentary settled it

Where the verse alone is ambiguous, the reading follows Śaṅkara's commentary; the deciding lines are quoted below each decision.
The privative compound is read literally as na ejat and is then defined as absence of kampana/calana, namely not falling away from its own state. This fixes the translation ‘unmoving’ and justifies adding the interpretive force ‘changeless.’
→ anejat means ‘unmoving, not shaking,’ i.e. changeless in its own nature, not merely physically still.
isup [1, 2]anejat na ejat / ejṛ kampane, kampanaṃ calanaṃ svāvasthāpracyutistadvarjitaṃ sarvadaikarūpamityarthaḥ /
‘Anejat’ means ‘not ejat.’ The root ejṛ means trembling; trembling is motion, falling away from one’s own state. Devoid of that, always of one form—this is the meaning.
The bhāṣya explicitly glosses manas as saṅkalpa/vikalpa/antaḥkaraṇa and explains why the self is called faster than it: when the mind swiftly goes even to Brahmaloka, the appearance of self-consciousness is grasped as if already there first.
→ manasaḥ is an ablative of comparison, and manas is the internal organ characterized by resolve and doubt; javīyaḥ means ‘swifter than the mind.’
isup [3]taccaikaṃ sarvabhūteṣu manasaḥ saṅkalpādilakṣaṇād javīyo javavattaram /
And that one in all beings is swifter than the mind, which is characterized by resolve and the like—more possessed of speed.
isup [6, 7]manaso 'ntaḥkaraṇasya saṅkalpavikalpalakṣaṇasyopādheranuvarttanād ... tasmin manasi brahmalokādīn drutaṃ gacchati sati prathamaṃ prāpta ivātmacaitanyāvabhāso gṛhyate 'to manaso javīya ityāha /
Because it follows the limiting adjunct, the mind, the internal organ characterized by resolve and doubt ... when that mind goes swiftly to Brahmaloka and the like, the appearance of self-consciousness is apprehended as if it had arrived first; therefore he says ‘swifter than the mind.’
Śaṅkara derives devāḥ from dyotana and identifies them with the eye and other senses. He glosses āpnuvan as prāptavantaḥ and says that even the mere appearance of the self does not become an object for the senses because their operation is mediated by the mind.
→ devāḥ are the sense-organs, not the gods as independent deities; na āpnuvan means ‘did not reach/attain/objectify this self-principle.’
isup [8, 9]nainaddevā dyotanāddevāścakṣurādīnīndriyāṇyetatprakṛtamātmatattvaṃ nāpnuvanna prāptavantaḥ / tebhyo mano javīyaḥ manovyāpāravyavahitatvād ābhāsamātramapi ātmano naiva devānāṃ viṣayībhavati /
‘The devāḥ’: because of illumination they are devāḥ, the eye and other sense-organs. They did not reach—did not attain—this self-principle now under discussion. The mind is swifter than them; because they are separated by the operation of the mind, not even a mere appearance of the self becomes an object for the senses.
The bhāṣya glosses the phrase as pūrvam eva gataṃ and grounds it in all-pervasiveness like space. It also states that worldly changes belong to adjuncts, while in its own unconditioned form the self is changeless.
→ pūrvam arṣat means ‘gone before beforehand,’ but this motion is only from the standpoint of limiting adjuncts; in itself the self is all-pervasive and changeless.
isup [10]yasmājjavanānmanaso 'pi pūrvamarṣat pūrvameva gataṃ vyomavadvyāpitvāt sarvavyāpi tadātmatattva sarvasaṃsāradharmavarjitaṃ svena nirupādhikena svarūpeṇāvikriyameva sadupādhikṛtāḥ sarvāḥ saṃsāravikriyā anubhavatītyavivekināṃ mūḍhānāmanekamiva ca pratidehaṃ pratyavabhāsata ityetadāha /
Because, before even the swift mind, it is gone before—already gone—since, being pervasive like space, that self-principle is all-pervasive, devoid of all worldly attributes, and, in its own unconditioned form, changeless; yet, for the undiscriminating and deluded, it experiences all worldly changes produced by adjuncts and appears as though many in each body. This is what he says.
Śaṅkara identifies the other runners and glosses atyeti as ‘as if passing beyond.’ He then says that tiṣṭhat itself shows the intended ‘as if’: the self itself is changeless.
→ dhāvato ’nyān atyeti tiṣṭhat means ‘standing still, it outstrips the other runners’; the ‘others’ are mind, speech, senses, etc., distinct from the self, and the outstripping is figurative.
isup [11, 12]taddhāvato drutaṃ gacchato 'nyānātmavilakṣaṇānmanovāgindriyaprabhṛtīnatyeti atītya gacchati iva / ivārthaṃ svayameva darśayati tiṣṭhaditi svayamavikriyameva sadityarthaḥ /
That, the others who run, who go swiftly—mind, speech, senses, and the like, distinct from the self—it outstrips, as though going beyond them. The word ‘standing’ itself shows the sense ‘as though’: the meaning is that it itself is simply changeless.
Śaṅkara glosses tasmin as tasminn ātmatattve sati, nityacaitanyasvabhāve, and concludes that all modifications of instruments and effects occur only when the eternal consciousness-self exists as their basis.
→ tasmin is locative in the self-principle as the ever-conscious substratum in which all activities occur.
isup [13]tasminnātmatattve sati nityacaitanyasvabhāve
When that self-principle exists, whose nature is eternal consciousness...
isup [17]sarvā hi kāryakaraṇādivikriyā nityacaitanyātmasvarūpe sarvāspadabhūte satyeva bhavantītyarthaḥ
For all modifications of effects, instruments, and so on occur only when the eternal-consciousness nature of the self exists as the basis of all.
Śaṅkara directly identifies mātariśvā with vāyu and elaborates it as the bearer of all living breaths, the activity-principle, the sūtra in which the aggregates of effects and instruments are woven.
→ mātariśvā is Vāyu, the life-bearing, activity-formed cosmic principle called sūtra, not primarily Agni in this passage.
isup [13]mātariśvā vāyuḥ sarvaprāṇabhṛt kriyātmako yadāśrayāṇi kāryakaraṇajātāni yasminnotāni protāni ca yatsūtrasaṃjñakaṃ sarvasya jagato vighārayitṛ sa mātariśvā
Mātariśvan is Vāyu, the bearer of all breaths, of the nature of activity; the aggregates of effects and instruments have it as support, are woven warp and woof in it, it is called the sūtra, the sustainer of the whole world—that is Mātariśvan.
Śaṅkara explicitly glosses apaḥ as karmāṇi and specifies both living beings’ movements and cosmic functions such as burning, shining, raining. He glosses dadhāti as vibhajati, then adds dhārayati as an alternative supported by śruti.
→ apaḥ is translated as ‘actions/works’ on Śaṅkara’s authority, and dadhāti primarily means ‘allots/distributes,’ with ‘supports’ as Śaṅkara’s alternative.
isup [13]apaḥ karmāṇi prāṇināṃ ceṣṭālakṣaṇāni, agnyādityaparjanyādīnāṃ jvalanadahanaprakāśābhivarṣaṇādilakṣaṇāni dadhāti vibhajati ityarthaḥ /
Apaḥ means actions: those of living beings, characterized as movements, and those of fire, sun, rain-cloud, and so on, characterized by blazing, burning, shining, raining, and the like. Dadhāti means ‘distributes/allots.’
isup [14, 15, 16]dhārayatīti vā"bhīṣāsmādvātaḥ pavate" (tai. u. 2 / 8 / 1) ityādi śrutibhyaḥ /
Or it means ‘supports,’ on the basis of scriptural passages such as ‘from fear of him the wind blows’ (Taittirīya Upaniṣad 2.8.1).
