Īśopaniṣad with Śaṅkara's bhāṣya · 5

tad ejati tan naijati tad dūre tad v antike | tad antar asya sarvasya tad u sarvasyāsya bāhyataḥ

Translation. That—the Self-reality (ātmatattva)—stirs, as it were; that does not stir in itself. That is far; that, too, is very near. That is within all this; that, too, is outside all this.

How the commentary settled it

Where the verse alone is ambiguous, the reading follows Śaṅkara's commentary; the deciding lines are quoted below each decision.
The pronoun is not translated as a merely loose deictic ‘that thing’; it points back to the already introduced reality of the Self. This governs the whole verse’s subject.
→ The repeated tat refers to the Self-reality, the ātmatattva under discussion.
isup [1]tadātmatattvaṃ yatprakṛtaṃ
‘That’ is the reality of the Self which is under discussion.
Although √ej can be rendered in more than one way, Śaṅkara glosses ejati with calati and immediately denies real motion from the Self’s own standpoint. Therefore the translation preserves the verbal contrast while marking the apparent character of the motion.
→ ejati is ‘stirs/moves’, but only ‘as it were’; na ejati means ‘does not move in itself’.
isup [1]tadejati calati tadeva ca naijati svato naiva calati svato 'calameva sat calatīvetyarthaḥ
‘That ejati’ means ‘it moves’; and that very one ‘does not ejati’: in itself it does not move at all; being in itself unmoving, it moves as it were—this is the meaning.
The far/near opposition is not merely spatial. Śaṅkara explains remoteness by unattainability for the unknowing, and nearness by being the very Self of knowers. The printed v is the sandhi-form of u before antike, as the commentary’s chheda states.
→ dūre and antike are predicative locatives: far for the ignorant, very near for knowers; tad v antike must be divided as tad u antike.
isup [2]kiñca taddūre varṣakoṭiśatairapyaviduṣāmaprāpyatvād dūra iva
Moreover, it is far: because for the unknowing it is unattainable even in hundreds of crores of years, it is as if far.
isup [3]tad u antike iti cchedaḥ
The division is: tad u antike.
isup [4]tadvantike samīpe 'tyantameva viduṣāmātmatvānna kevalaṃ dūre 'ntike ca
That is near, very close indeed, because it is the Self of knowers; not only far, but near as well.
antar is taken as an indeclinable ‘within/inside’ governing the genitive asya sarvasya, not as a separate nominal adjective. Śaṅkara glosses it with abhyantare and expands the genitive as ‘of this whole world whose nature is name, form, and action’.
→ antar asya sarvasya means ‘within all this’, i.e. within this whole world of name, form, and action.
isup [5, 6, 7]tadantarabhyantare 'sya sarvasya"ya ātmā sarvāntaraḥ"(bṛ. u. 3 / 4 / 1) iti śruteḥ asya sarvasya jagato nāma rūpakriyātmakasya
That is antar, inside, of all this, according to the śruti ‘the Self that is innermost in all’; of all this world whose nature is name, form, and action.
Śaṅkara reads the particle with api and explains exteriority through all-pervasiveness, like space. Thus bāhyataḥ is not a nominal case-form but an adverbial/postpositional indeclinable taking sarvasya asya as its genitive complement.
→ tad u sarvasyāsya bāhyataḥ means ‘that too is outside all this’, with bāhyataḥ governing the genitive and u carrying an ‘also/too’ force.
isup [7]tadu api sarvasya asya bāhyato vyāpakatvādākāśavanniratiśayasūkṣmatvād antaḥ
That too is outside all this, because of pervasiveness, like space; and inside because of unsurpassed subtlety.
